\section{\cluethreed{} performance with pileup}
\label{sec:pileup}

Pileup introduces many nearby showers with a broad range of energies and
particle types. I studied samples with an average of 200 additional interactions per bunch
crossing (PU200), keeping two efficiencies apart: the efficiency of
reconstructing the injected signal photon, and the inclusive efficiency
measured over all selected in-time SimTracksters (signal plus pileup). Signal-only curve tests the same displaced-photon topology in a busier
environment. Inclusive curve also changes the population being
measured.

\subsection{Signal efficiency and the meaning of displacement}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/pileup_efficiency.png}
  \caption{Default \cluethreed{} efficiency without pileup, for the generated
  signal photon in PU200, and for the inclusive in-time truth selection in
  PU200.}
  \label{fig:pileupefficiency}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:pileupefficiency} compares the no-pileup photon sample, the
signal photon in PU200, and the inclusive PU200 selection. The first two
curves are similar: both have high efficiency at small displacement and
show the large-$\R$ degradation identified in
Section~\ref{sec:singlephoton}. The inclusive curve is quite different, and it
is dominated by pileup: the signal photon accounts for fewer than $1$ in
$1000$ of SimTracksters, so the inclusive denominator is
essentially the pileup population. It
falls sharply just outside the first displacement bin, reaches a minimum,
and then increases over a broad range of $\R$. A comparison of the Trackster and TICL-candidate efficiencies also
showed no substantial change in this behaviour.

For pileup particles, resolved $\R$ is particularly easy to misinterpret. The
HGCal boundary position and direction define a line that can be extrapolated to
$z=0$. Charged particles bend in the magnetic field, so this line is a tangent
to a curved trajectory. Its $R$ can be far from 0 even when
the particle was produced close to beamline. The
reconstruction consequences, however, are the same either way: a trajectory
with a large intercept still projects poorly onto the interaction point, so by
Eq.~\eqref{eq:displacedscale} its LayerClusters are pushed apart as
$d_{XY}\simeq\R|\Delta z|/z$ and suffer the same fragmentation as a genuinely
displaced shower.

Even for a straight photon trajectory, the longitudinal spread of collision
vertices matters. For a PU photon produced on the beamline at $z=z_v$, the
transverse intercept of its line at $z=0$ has magnitude
\begin{equation}
 R=|z_v|\tan\theta,
 \label{eq:pileupphotonR}
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ is the momentum polar angle relative to the beam axis.

\subsection{Separating particle composition from reconstruction effects}

To investigate the inclusive shape, I broke the efficiency down per particle
in a 20\,000-event study.

\begin{figure*}[!tp]
  \centering
  \begin{subfigure}{0.49\textwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/pileup_composition.png}
    \caption{Composition of the in-time truth denominator.}
  \end{subfigure}\hfill
  \begin{subfigure}{0.49\textwidth}
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/pileup_efficiency_pid.png}
    \caption{Efficiency split by particle species.}
  \end{subfigure}
  \caption{Particle composition and reconstruction efficiency versus resolved
  $\R$ in pileup.}
  \label{fig:pileuppid}
\end{figure*}

The truth composition changes rapidly near $\R=0$. Photons contribute
strongly in the first bins since they are not curved by magnetic field, while charged pions dominate most of the larger
resolved-displacement range (Fig.~\ref{fig:pileuppid}). This accounts for part
of the change, but it is not a complete explanation: efficiencies
within individual species still depend on $\R$.

For photons, dependence at small $\R$ is angular rather
than on displacement. Pileup photons come from vertices close to the beamline,
so $z_v$ is small and Eq.~\eqref{eq:pileupphotonR} makes resolved
$\R=|z_v|\tan\theta$ essentially a proxy for polar angle. A two-dimensional
efficiency map in $(\R,|\eta|)$ confirms this, with the structure changing in
$|\eta|$ rather than $\R$. Loss is present in the lower-$|\eta|$ edge of
the HGCAL acceptance; a one-dimensional photon-versus-$\R$ curve inherits
that acceptance edge and only looks like a displacement effect.

For charged pions, kaons and protons the efficiency rises with
resolved $\R$, and the explaining variable
is the LC multiplicity of the truth shower: showers with many LCs rarely have
more than half of their energy collected in a single Trackster. As $\R$ grows
the whole pion LC-multiplicity distribution shifts downward, so the population
moves towards the smaller, few-LC showers that \cluethreed{} reconstructs more
easily, which was not fully explained why. Higher-momentum hadrons bend less, keeping a smaller resolved $\R$
while developing larger showers, so larger $\R$ preferentially selects the
lower-multiplicity ones.

Inclusive pileup efficiency must therefore be read together with particle
identity, energy, angle and shower size, and the signal-only selection remains
the test of displaced-photon reconstruction in pileup environment.
