\section{Comparison with \cluestering{}}
\label{sec:cluestering}

\cluestering{} is a generalized, heterogeneous implementation of the CLUE
approach, with configurable coordinates and distance metrics, meaning projective coordinates are only one of the options. It retains the
density, nearest-higher and seed--follower structure introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:clue3d}. My work was to run and diagnose its application to displaced showers
in CMSSW, comparing several configurations with default \cluethreed{}.

\subsection{Cartesian distance metrics}

For Cartesian coordinates $(x,y,z)$ I tested three distance families. Writing
$\Delta x=x_i-x_j$, and similarly for $y$ and $z$, their unweighted forms are
\begin{align}
 D_2 &= \sqrt{\Delta x^2+\Delta y^2+\Delta z^2},\\
 D_1 &= |\Delta x|+|\Delta y|+|\Delta z|,\\
 D_\infty &= \max(|\Delta x|,|\Delta y|,|\Delta z|).
\end{align}
A fixed cut selects a sphere for the Euclidean distance $D_2$, an octahedron for
the Manhattan distance $D_1$ and a cube for the Chebyshev distance $D_\infty$. Positive weights
$w_k$ on the coordinate differences $\Delta x_k$ adjust the reach per axis,
\begin{align}
 D_{2,w} &= \sqrt{\sum_k w_k(\Delta x_k)^2},\\
 D_{1,w} &= \sum_k w_k|\Delta x_k|,\\
 D_{\infty,w} &= \max_k\bigl(w_k|\Delta x_k|\bigr),
\end{align}
so a larger $w_z$ shortens the longitudinal reach. All these metrics depend only on coordinate
differences and make no projection towards the interaction point.

\subsection{A projective metric under development}

I also tested a work-in-progress projective configuration, labelled
\emph{Gnomonic} in the plots. It maps LC positions to the direction coordinates
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf{g}=\left(\frac{x}{|z|},\frac{y}{|z|}\right).
\end{equation}
It uses the layer index $\ell$ as the longitudinal coordinate, and applies the cylinder distance
\begin{equation}
 D_{\mathrm{cyl}}(i,j)=\max\!\left(
 \frac{|\mathbf{g}_i-\mathbf{g}_j|}{\sigma_T},
 \frac{|\ell_i-\ell_j|}{L}\right).
 \label{eq:cluesteringcylinder}
\end{equation}
Pointing showers have constant $\mathbf{g}$; non-pointing ones drift across this
plane, so some degradation with displacement is still expected. The tested
implementation clusters only the electromagnetic section (EE), with
$\sigma_T=0.003$, $L=4$ layers, density radius $d_c=1$, seed distance 2.8 and
outlier distance 3.6 in the normalized units of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cluesteringcylinder}. Its density threshold varies with direction
to match the $\eta$-uniform pileup density,
\begin{equation}
 \rho_c(\mathbf{g})=\SI{0.8}{GeV}
       \left(\frac{0.42}{|\mathbf{g}|}\right)^2.
\end{equation}

\subsection{First comparisons without pileup}

These are exploratory tests, not the result of tuning. For the Cartesian
configurations I held the density radius at $d_c=\SI{5}{cm}$ and the density threshold
at $\rho_c=\SI{6}{GeV}$ and varied the nearest-higher distance $d_m$, identified in
the previous studies as the most direct control of fragmentation. The sample is 20\,000
single-photon events with energies of \SI{10--250}{GeV} and $\R=\SI{0--350}{cm}$ in
Run4D121 geometry, subject to the gun's surface-acceptance requirements.

The \SI{6}{cm} Cartesian configurations largely avoid the severe large-displacement
loss of \cluethreed{} (Fig.~\ref{fig:cluesteringefficiencies}). The
gnomonic configuration degrades at large displacement, as expected for
projective coordinates, but far less than \cluethreed{}, so it too looks
promising--- origin projection does not imply identical performance,
since the cylinder neighbourhood, distance scales and variable density all
differ. The surprise is the smaller Euclidean setting: weighted \SI{3}{cm} loses almost all efficiency at approximately \SI{300}{cm}.
Since it uses Cartesian coordinates, this failure cannot be attributed to an
origin projection.

\subsection{Understanding the weighted Euclidean \SI{3}{cm} failure}



\begin{figure*}[!tp]
  \centering
  \begin{subfigure}{\textwidth}
    \cmsplot[width=\linewidth]{figures/cluestering_signal_pu.png}
    \caption{Efficiency for the generated signal photon in PU200.}
    \label{fig:cluesteringsignalpu}
  \end{subfigure}
  \par\medskip
  \begin{subfigure}{\textwidth}
    \cmsplot[width=\linewidth]{figures/cluestering_inclusive_pu.png}
    \caption{Efficiency for the inclusive in-time truth population.}
    \label{fig:cluesteringinclusive}
  \end{subfigure}
  \caption{PU200 efficiencies, with default \cluethreed{} as the baseline
  and the same relative-efficiency convention as
  Fig.~\ref{fig:cluesteringefficiencies}.}
  \label{fig:cluesteringpuefficiencies}
\end{figure*}

To understand why, I plotted coordinate components of the nearest-higher
links and the photon angle (Fig.~\ref{fig:cluesteringdiagnostic}). For a layer gap $\Delta z$, transverse step is
\begin{equation}
 \Delta r_{xy}\simeq|\Delta z|\tan\theta,
 \label{eq:cluesteringangle}
\end{equation}
where $\theta$ is the photon polar angle. The layer spacing makes almost constant the
longitudinal part of each link, while a larger $\theta$ adds transverse
distance; panel~(a) shows exactly this, the transverse components growing with
$\R$ while the longitudinal one barely changes. Once the full distance
outgrows the \SI{3}{cm} reach the link is cut and shower is split. The failure is thus not
inherent to displaced particles but to large-$\theta$ ones, which displaced
particles tend to be.

Clustering acts on individual links, so an average reach under the \SI{3}{cm}
threshold can still break many links above it. Below about \SI{100}{cm} the mean photon
angle falls, yet the most inclined fifth of the photons moves to larger $\theta$
(panel~b).

The lesson is that Cartesian coordinates remove the artificial projective separation, but the distance reach must still be large enough to follow an inclined shower from one
layer to the next.

\subsection{Performance in pileup}

The PU200 comparison uses a 10\,000-event sample for each tested
configuration. As in Section~\ref{sec:pileup}, I measured the generated signal
photon separately from the inclusive in-time truth population.

For the signal photon, the no-pileup ordering survives
(Fig.~\ref{fig:cluesteringsignalpu}): the \SI{6}{cm} Cartesian configurations stay
efficient across the scan, the gnomonic configuration again beats
\cluethreed{} at large displacement, and the weighted Euclidean \SI{3}{cm} failure
is still present.

The inclusive picture is different (Fig.~\ref{fig:cluesteringinclusive}). The
\SI{6}{cm} Cartesian metrics remain competitive---unweighted Euclidean improves on
\cluethreed{} over most of the range and weighted Chebyshev is broadly
comparable---while Manhattan and the \SI{3}{cm} choices recover much less. The
gnomonic configuration is the outlier: even though its signal
efficiency is the higher of the two, it collects only a small fraction
of the inclusive population, several times less than \cluethreed{} does.

This is unlikely to be the projective metric itself: its direction-dependent
density threshold probably suppresses much of the pileup, and it does not yet
cluster the hadronic section, which matters for PU. Both remain to be confirmed.

\subsection{Two nearby photons and outlook}



A dedicated two-photon comparison at \SI{4}{cm} separation tests nearby showers
directly (Fig.~\ref{fig:cluesteringtwophoton}). Here efficiency cannot be read
on its own: two overlapping photons collected into a single object still count
as reconstructed, so a high efficiency can just as easily mean good recovery or
outright merging. On that
basis the gnomonic configuration holds up well: at large displacement it keeps
higher efficiency than \cluethreed{} while its merge rate stays low and close
to \cluethreed{}'s. It recovers more of each photon without merging the two
together.

Unweighted Euclidean
at $d_m=\SI{6}{cm}$ reaches near-perfect efficiency, but its merge rate is very high:
the wide reach simply clusters both showers into one object, which is what made
it look so strong on single photons. More surprising is $d_m=\SI{3}{cm}$, whose short
reach fragmented a single shower earlier around 0.8 efficiency: with two photons its efficiency climbs
back to near 1 while it merges almost as heavily as the \SI{6}{cm} case. The
likely explanation is that activity from the neighbouring shower bridges the
spacing a \SI{3}{cm} reach cannot span alone and increases density, reconnecting the fragments into one
merged object, though this would need a dedicated study to confirm.

As shown in Section~\ref{sec:merging}, fragmentation can also hide mixing
inside an object-based merge rate, so extending the cross-shower LC link
diagnostic to this experiment would test merging more directly. Taken
together, the results show that \cluestering{} offers several useful choices:
the gnomonic configuration improves on \cluethreed{} in both single- and
two-photon tests, while the Cartesian metrics buy their high efficiency partly
by merging and must be tuned against that. The next
step is to tune coordinates, metric weights,
distance reaches and density settings together, using signal and PU efficiency, duplicate, merge and fake rate to guide the choice.
