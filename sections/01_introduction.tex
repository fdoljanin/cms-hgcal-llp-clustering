\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The year 2031 will mark the start of the High-Luminosity phase of the Large
Hadron Collider (HL-LHC)~\cite{HLLHC}, which will deliver proton--proton
collisions at an unprecedented instantaneous luminosity. The resulting data
volume and the severe pileup---of order $200$ simultaneous interactions per
bunch crossing---demand a new generation of reconstruction algorithms. To cope
with these conditions the CMS experiment will replace its endcap calorimeters
with the High-Granularity Calorimeter (HGCAL)~\cite{HGCALTDR}, a sampling
calorimeter with roughly six million channels arranged in about fifty
longitudinal layers. Its fine transverse and longitudinal segmentation records
the detailed three-dimensional structure of electromagnetic and hadronic
showers, but it also makes traditional calorimeter clustering impractical and
motivates dedicated pattern-recognition algorithms.

The Clustering of Energy (CLUE) algorithm~\cite{Rovere2020clue} was developed for
exactly this purpose: it is a fast, parallel, density-based clustering algorithm
that groups HGCAL energy deposits into physics objects such as photons and
electrons. In the CMS reconstruction, CLUE and its three-dimensional extension
\cluethreed{} sit inside The Iterative Clustering (TICL)
framework~\cite{TICL}, which builds objects in stages
(Fig.~\ref{fig:rechits}): individual calorimeter hits (\emph{RecHits}) are first
grouped by CLUE into per-layer two-dimensional \emph{LayerClusters} (LCs);
\cluethreed{} then links LayerClusters across layers into three-dimensional
\emph{Tracksters}; and a subsequent linking step associates Tracksters into the
final TICL candidates. The Monte Carlo truth is described by an analogous chain,
in which a generated particle (\emph{CaloParticle}) is mapped to a
\emph{SimTrackster} that collects the LayerClusters truly belonging to it.
Reconstruction performance is quantified by comparing reconstructed Tracksters to
SimTracksters through their shared energy, as detailed below.

A key assumption underlies \cluethreed{}: the neighbour search that links layer
clusters across layers is performed in a coordinate system \emph{projected from
the interaction point}, so that a shower travelling radially outward
from the detector centre keeps a fixed transverse position layer to layer. This
holds for prompt particles produced at the beamline, but it doesn't need to for
the class of \emph{long-lived particles} (LLPs) predicted by many extensions of
the Standard Model~\cite{LLPreview}. Rather than decaying within a few
millimetres of the collision point, an LLP can traverse a sizeable fraction of
the detector before decaying, producing \emph{displaced}, non-pointing
particles. A representative signature is a hidden-valley dark shower, in which
long-lived dark hadrons decay to Standard Model photons far from the beamline. Because these showers do not point back to
the origin, they may violate exactly the assumption on which \cluethreed{}
relies.

The goal of this project is to determine
whether CLUE is capable of clustering the energy deposits arising from such
novel topologies, to quantify the expected performance reduction as a function
of the distance from the detector centre at which the LLP decays, and to explore an alternative algorithm. We restrict the study to
neutral \emph{photons}: photons are not deflected by the CMS magnetic field, so
their trajectories are straight lines and their displacement and incidence angle
are unambiguously defined; they also do not interact in the tracker before
reaching the calorimeter. This isolates the calorimeter clustering behaviour
from tracking and magnetic-field effects and provides the cleanest possible test
of the origin assumption.

The remainder of this report is organized as follows. We first define the
displacement observables and describe how they are reconstructed for objects that
have no associated truth vertex, then present the displaced-photon Monte Carlo
production and the performance metrics. After reviewing how \cluethreed{} works,
with emphasis on the origin assumption, we present the single-photon efficiency,
the two-shower merging behaviour, and the performance under pileup, before
comparing \cluethreed{} against the alternative algorithm \cluestering{} and
concluding.
