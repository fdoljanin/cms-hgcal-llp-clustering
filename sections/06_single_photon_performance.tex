\section{CLUE3D performance for displaced photons}
\label{sec:singlephoton}

The central question of this project is whether \cluethreed{} can reconstruct
a photon shower whose trajectory does not point back to the interaction
point. The single-photon study reveals both performance loss
and an unexpected structure: the loss develops in two stages, separated by a
plateau. The evidence identifies fragmentation induced by the
origin-based projection as the mechanism behind the observed loss.

\subsection{The performance loss is not a smooth decline}

The mechanism study uses single displaced photons without pileup, with Run4D121
geometry and the default HLT \cluethreed{} configuration. The wide-range
production samples $\R$ uniformly from $0$ to $\SI{350}{cm}$ and photon
energy from $10$ to $\SI{250}{GeV}$, subject to the production- and
target-surface constraints described in Section~\ref{sec:gun}. The initial
investigation was used 50\,000-events, one event containing one displaced photon. Individual diagnostic
plots impose their own requirements, stated where relevant, rather than
necessarily containing identical numbers of objects.

In addition to the efficiency of Section~\ref{sec:metrics}, I recorded the
largest fraction of each SimTrackster's energy captured by any one
reconstructed Trackster,
\begin{equation}
  f_{\max}(S)=\max_T\frac{E_{\mathrm{shared}}(S,T)}{E_S},
  \label{eq:fmax}
\end{equation}
with zero assigned when there is no associated reconstructed object.
Efficiency is the fraction of SimTracksters with $f_{\max}>0.5$; the mean
$\langle f_{\max}\rangle$ measures energy recovery continuously, without
reducing each shower to a pass or fail.

Figure~\ref{fig:singlephotonperformance} shows that the standard efficiency is
close to unity at small displacement, falls to roughly $85\%$ in the
intermediate region, and reaches about $20\%$ near the end of the scan.
The mean captured fraction makes the structure particularly clear:
it stays near $0.92$ up to approximately $\R=\SI{180}{cm}$, falls to
about $0.78$ over $180$--$\SI{220}{cm}$, remains near that value to about
$\SI{270}{cm}$, and then falls steeply to roughly $0.38$ near
$\SI{350}{cm}$.

The intermediate plateau was the main puzzle. Increasing the displacement
should progressively violate the pointing assumption, yet the reconstruction
temporarily stops deteriorating. Varying the
shared-energy requirement changes the numerical efficiency and the visibility
of the plateau. However, the two-stage loss remains in the continuous mean
$f_{\max}$, so it cannot be explained by the choice of the $0.5$
efficiency threshold.

\subsection{Testing geometric explanations}

  Trackster and marker size the energy. Outlined markers belong to the target

  assignment changes repeatedly.}
  \label{fig:singlephotonsplit}

I first investigated whether the limited $(\eta,\phi)$ tile search could lose
LayerClusters from the same shower, since a displaced shower does not keep a
constant $(\eta,\phi)$ from layer to layer and its LayerClusters could drift out
of the search window. The event displays and distributions of
layer-to-layer angular steps did not support this as the main explanation.
Across the full displacement scan, even the largest single-layer step of a
shower stays well below the tile reach (Fig.~\ref{fig:layerstep}), so the
neighbour always falls inside the tile search. The distinction matters: the
candidate can be found by the tile search and still be too distant under the
projected metric.

Figure~\ref{fig:singlephotonsplit} illustrates this directly. At
$\R\simeq\SI{297}{cm}$, one SimTrackster is split into five reconstructed
Tracksters, and the largest shared-energy fraction is only $0.21$. The
successive colours follow different longitudinal pieces of a continuous
shower core. Several changes of colour occur while the core remains within
one $\phi$ tile. This is fragmentation of one shower, not five independently
produced photons, and---consistent with the sample-wide picture of
Fig.~\ref{fig:layerstep}---no tile boundary is crossed.

A second possibility was that scalar $\R$ mixes different trajectory
orientations. I separated photons by the azimuthal difference between their
back-extrapolated origin and their position at the target plane. The aligned
and oppositely oriented populations do have different captured fractions at
the same $\R$, but the plateau survives within the aligned population
(Fig.~\ref{fig:singlephotonmixing}). Mixing these populations therefore does
not create the feature. Binning in $\alphainc$ also reveals a short plateau,
around $41^\circ$--$44^\circ$, followed by a strong decline. A further split
into broad $\alphainc$ ranges retained the flat-$\R$ region within the
$29^\circ\leq\alphainc<49^\circ$ group (Fig.~\ref{fig:singlephotonalpharegime}).
These checks show that the feature
survives changes of projection and sample subdivision, while also establishing
that $\R$ alone does not specify every aspect of the trajectory.

\subsection{The geometric origin of the growing distance}

The projected distance of Section~\ref{sec:clue3d} suggests a direct mechanism.
For a straight trajectory on the positive endcap, write its transverse
position as
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}_{\perp}(z)=\mathbf{b}+z\mathbf{a},
  \qquad \R=|\mathbf{b}|,
  \label{eq:displacedline}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{a}$ is the transverse slope and $\mathbf{b}$ the intercept at
$z=0$. For a pointing trajectory $\mathbf{b}=0$: both $r/z$ and $\phi$ are
constant along the ideal shower axis. For a displaced trajectory,
$\mathbf{x}_{\perp}(z)/z=\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}/z$ changes with depth, as the
$r/z$ panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:singlephotonsplit} demonstrates for a real shower.

Projecting a point on layer $j$ to layer $i$ along a ray from the interaction
point gives
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{proj}}_{j\to i}
    =\frac{z_i}{z_j}\mathbf{x}_{\perp}(z_j).
\end{equation}
Subtracting this projected position from the true position on layer $i$
cancels the slope term exactly:
\begin{align}
  \Delta\mathbf{x}_{ij}
    &=\mathbf{x}_{\perp}(z_i)-\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{proj}}_{j\to i}\\
    &=\mathbf{b}\left(1-\frac{z_i}{z_j}\right)
     =\mathbf{b}\frac{z_j-z_i}{z_j}.
  \label{eq:projectionerror}
\end{align}
Thus the exact Cartesian offset between ideal axis points is
$|\Delta\mathbf{x}_{ij}|=\R|z_j-z_i|/z_j$. For neighbouring layers, and
using the small-angle transverse metric implemented in \cluethreed{},
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{d_{XY}(i,j)\simeq\R\frac{|\Delta z|}{z}.}
  \label{eq:displacedscale}
\end{equation}
There is a distinction between the exact vector relation and the approximate
$d_{XY}$ relation: the code uses radial and azimuthal
components, and actual LayerClusters fluctuate around the ideal axis.

Equation~\eqref{eq:displacedscale} explains why the distance grows with both
displacement and layer separation even for a perfectly straight photon.
It also gives the approximate scale at which a link exceeds the critical
distance,
\begin{equation}
  \R_{\mathrm{crit}}\simeq\frac{\dm z}{|\Delta z|}.
  \label{eq:criticaldisplacement}
\end{equation}
This supplies a prediction to test against the observed regions. It does not
by itself predict the full efficiency: whether a particular LC becomes a new
seed also depends on its local density, its self-energy fraction and the
available higher-density candidates.

The first comparison with the layer-gap distances looked particularly
promising. I selected the most energetic LC on each layer,
retained it if its energy exceeded $\SI{1}{GeV}$, and compared pairs separated
by $d=1,2,3$ layers. SimTracksters were required to contain at least four LCs.
In the radial component alone, the mean $d=3$ offset reaches
$\SI{1.8}{cm}$ near the first performance decline, and the mean $d=2$ offset
reaches it near the final decline (Fig.~\ref{fig:singlephotonradial}). This
suggested an appealing explanation in which successively shorter links were
lost as displacement increased.

However, that particular assignment of the two declines does not survive
using the actual distance in the algorithm. The azimuthal term also
contributes to $d_{XY}$; it is not consistently negligible. Once both
components are included, the crossing positions change. The radial-only alignment was therefore insufficient evidence: the relevant
comparison had to use the full projected distance and identify which
higher-density neighbours were associated with actual new seeds.

The local displays illustrate why the full distance matters. In
Fig.~\ref{fig:singlephotonradial}(b), the marked same-shower LC on the
preceding layer lies $\SI{1.82}{cm}$ from the new fragment's core in
projected coordinates, just outside the critical circle. This shows how
neighbouring pieces of a continuous shower can fall on opposite sides of the
distance criterion.

  (a) Mean full projected distance for the same core-LC pairs as in the
  radial-only comparison. (b) Full reconstructed energy in Tracksters whose
  target-owned seed has a higher-density candidate beyond $\SI{1.8}{cm}$,
  normalized to the SimTrackster energy and separated by the layer gap $d$ to
  that candidate. The $d=2$ crossing in (a) coincides with the sharp rise of

  \label{fig:singlephotongaps}

The decisive connection is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:singlephotongaps}.
Near $\R\simeq\SI{200}{cm}$, the mean full projected distance for
$d=2$ pairs crosses $\SI{1.8}{cm}$. At the same time, the energy carried by reco
Tracksters which failed to follow nearestHigher $d=2$ layers away rises sharply. This connects the geometric threshold to the objects that
actually remove energy from the best reconstructed match.

Panel (b) isolates those fragments: for each reconstructed Trackster whose seed lies beyond the $\SI{1.8}{cm}$ threshold from
its higher-density neighbour, I sum the full Trackster energy---the seed and all
its followers---and normalize it to the shower energy in each $\R$ bin,
separated by the layer gap $d$ to that neighbour.

The two-layer contribution rises from almost zero to roughly $15\%$ of the
reference shower energy through the first decline. It then levels off over
approximately the same displacement range as the plateau in
$\langle f_{\max}\rangle$. The plateau therefore corresponds to a region
where this newly important source of fragmentation has largely stabilized,
before the next one becomes dominant. At larger displacement, the green
$d=1$ contribution rises rapidly and drives the second, stronger loss of
captured energy.

This also explains why the distance plot alone was not enough. Three-layer
pairs cross $\SI{1.8}{cm}$ earliest, but add little seeded energy, because a
closer higher-density neighbour can still catch the LayerCluster and prevent a
split. And although the mean one-layer distance stays below $\SI{1.8}{cm}$ for
the whole scan, individual one-layer links still exceed it and produce the
observed contribution. The distance plot therefore only sets the geometric
scale; it is the seed-resolved energy that shows which crossings actually cause
fragmentation, and together they explain the first decline, the plateau and the
second decline.

\subsection{A controlled change of the critical distance}

To test whether this decision actually causes the loss, I reran the HLT
reconstruction on the same sample with the critical distance raised from
$\dm=\SI{1.8}{cm}$ to $\SI{2.1}{cm}$, leaving the density radius at
$\dc=\SI{1.8}{cm}$ (the outlier distance, twice $\dm$, rises automatically to
$\SI{4.2}{cm}$).

The point is not that a larger $\dm$ gives higher efficiency, but that the same
explanation should still predict \emph{where} the degradation happens. For
$\dm=\SI{2.1}{cm}$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:criticaldisplacement} places the $d=2$
threshold crossing at a larger displacement, so that stage of fragmentation
should set in later---and it does: the two-layer seeded contribution turns on at
larger $\R$, with the captured fraction and efficiency changing
accordingly (Fig.~\ref{fig:singlephotonablation}(a)).

This explains the mechanism. The origin-based projection pushes apart LCs of the
same displaced shower; as displacement grows, their projected distance crosses
$\dm$ in stages, creating extra seeds whose followers
become separate Tracksters and drain energy from the best match. Changing $\dm$
moves that fragmentation to exactly the displacement the same equation
predicts---so the loss is caused by the parameter the geometry implicated, not
merely correlated with displacement.

This is a mechanism test, not a tuning of \cluethreed{}: a larger distance can
also merge activity from different showers, so it must be checked with nearby
photons and pileup. Still, the single-photon study pins the degradation to a
concrete mismatch between the shower geometry and the algorithm's origin-based
distance.
