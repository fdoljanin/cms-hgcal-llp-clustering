\section{Conclusions and next steps}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This project studied how well \cluethreed{} reconstructs displaced photons
and why its performance changes when their showers do not point back to the
interaction point. Along the way I improved the displaced-particle gun, added displacement observables
to the CMSSW validation, and produced the two-dimensional performance maps used throughout. Comparing
shower direction estimators gave an approximately \SI{10}{cm} resolution on the
back-extrapolated displacement of the most energetic Trackster.
The default PCA estimator already available in CMSSW achieved the best resolution; several alternative methods reached comparable performance, but none improved upon it.

A large part of the work went into explaining why default \cluethreed{} loses efficiency on
single displaced photons. Because it projects everything from the origin, two points
on the same shower axis look transversely separated by roughly $R|\Delta z|/z$,
and this separation grows with displacement. The loss comes in two
stages: in the first, nearest-higher link 2 layer away breaks, so efficiency decreases and then plateaus, and at larger displacement one-layer breaks take over
and drive the final decline. Widening the seed distance from
\SI{1.8}{cm} to \SI{2.1}{cm}, pushes the
fragmentation out to larger displacement confirming the theory.

The two-photon studies make the case that energy recovery cannot be judged on
its own. For photons \SI{4}{cm} apart, the energy linked across the two showers
keeps rising with displacement even where the object-based merge rate is
falling, so fragmentation can make the merge rate look better while the mixing
underneath is getting worse. In PU200 the injected signal photon behaves much
as it does without pileup. The inclusive truth population is a different story:
its particle mix, kinematics and shower sizes all shift with resolved
displacement, and charged particles bend in the magnetic field, so its
displacement R cannot be read as a production radius.

Comparing metrics through \cluestering{} showed both promise and limits.
Several Cartesian configurations stay efficient on single displaced photons
across the whole scan, including in pileup, but the Euclidean ones merge the
\SI{4}{cm} photon pair heavily. The tested gnomonic configuration improved
efficiency over default \cluethreed{} at large displacement while keeping the
two-photon merge rate low. Further tuning is needed before deciding which
approach performs best overall. Its EE-only coverage may also contribute
to its low inclusive pileup efficiency.

The natural next step is to tune coordinates, metric weights, distance scales
and density thresholds together, judging them against energy recovery,
fragmentation and cross-shower mixing at once. \cluestering{} also needs to be tested on more
realistic inputs: the controlled photons here are injected just before HGCAL,
so they say nothing about real LLP decays. The
generation and validation tools built for this study give a starting point for
that work. We would also need to establish with theory colleagues the
maximum extrapolated displacement expected for relevant LLP scenarios. Since
default \cluethreed{} performs well up to approximately \SI{100}{cm} for current params in this
study, it may already be sufficient.
