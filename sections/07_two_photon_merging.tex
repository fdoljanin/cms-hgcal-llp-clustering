\section{Merging in two-photon events}
\label{sec:merging}

The single-photon study looked at fragmentation, but a useful reconstruction
must also keep neighbouring showers separate. Increasing the nearestHigher distance
can make displaced shower efficient, while merging multiple of them.
I therefore extended the displaced-gun studies to two photons. All results in this section use the default \cluethreed{} configuration.

\subsection{Controlled shower separation}

The two photons are separated with a distance-scan generator: they have equal
energies and parallel momenta, with production points separated by a distance
$s=\SI{4}{cm}$.
This separation was chosen because the merging behaviour is most clearly visible
there. Other values of $s$ were also studied, as shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:merge-distance-scan}, but are not discussed further.
The study uses 50\,000 generated events.

Merge rate initially appears encouraging at large $\R$: after rising at intermediate displacement, it falls again
(Fig.~\ref{fig:mergeratedistance}). However, this is the same region in which
single showers fragment strongly. Fragmentation can break an object that matched
two showers into smaller pieces that no longer pass both association cuts for merge, and also increases total number of tracksters.
A decreasing merge rate therefore does not establish that the algorithm has
become better at separating the photons.

\subsection{A diagnostic based on the clustering links}

To better measure merging, I introduced a
\emph{merge index} that measures cross-shower mixing directly from the
LayerCluster links, without depending on how many Tracksters are produced. Every
follower LC $i$ has a nearest-higher neighbour $h(i)$. If $h(i)$ is owned by
a different truth shower than $i$, the algorithm has linked across two showers, which ideally should not happen. The merge index is the energy-weighted fraction of follower energy in
such links,
\begin{equation}
 I_{\mathrm{merge}}(B)=
 \frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{F}_B}E_i\,
 [o(h(i))\ne o(i)]}
 {\sum_{i\in\mathcal{F}_B}E_i},
 \label{eq:mergeindex}
\end{equation}
where $o(\cdot)$ is the LC's truth shower, $E_i$ is LC energy, and $\mathcal{F}_B$
 are follower LCs whose truth shower is in
displacement bin $B$.

The index measures energy-weighted LayerClusters linked to wrong nearestHigher, instead of the total energy eventually
assigned to the wrong shower. One crossing link can redirect a whole chain of
followers, and seed and outlier decisions change which LCs enter the
denominator. These differences make it complementary to the object-based merge
rate. Unlike merge rate, this diagnostic is not generic: it requires a nearest-higher link structure and therefore applies only to CLUE-like algorithms.

Figure~\ref{fig:mergeindexdistance} denies the apparent improvement. For
$s=\SI{4}{cm}$, the index rises from close to zero near the origin to about $0.18$
at large displacement. Cross-shower linking becomes more frequent even while
the reconstructed-object merge rate decreases.

\subsection{Connection to the projective geometry}
\begin{figure}[!tp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/merge_mechanism_rz.png}
  \caption{An actual cross-shower nearest-higher link for photons separated by
  $s=\SI{4}{cm}$ at $\R\simeq\SI{131}{cm}$, shown as $r/z$ versus layer. Blue marks the
   simulated truth shower $A$ and red the simulated truth shower $B$, rather
  than reconstructed Trackster membership; the dashed line is the
  recorded nearest-higher hop from $B$'s highlighted LC (layer 55) to $A$'s
  (layer 52). The two lie at nearly the same $r/z$ despite belonging to
  different showers.}
  \label{fig:mergemechanism}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:mergemechanism} shows an individual cross-shower link, in an
event with $\R\simeq\SI{131}{cm}$. The highlighted red LC belongs to
shower $B$, but its recorded nearest-higher neighbour is the blue LC from
shower $A$, three layers away. The link is geometrically possible because the
two LCs sit at nearly the same $r/|z|$ and $\phi$ despite belonging to different
showers, so once projected onto the same layer the neighbour falls within the
critical distance.

The origin projection discussed in Section~\ref{sec:singlephoton} can also
explain how links cross between the showers. Consider ideal parallel axes
$\mathbf{r}_A(z)=\mathbf{b}_A+z\mathbf{a}$ and
$\mathbf{r}_B(z)=\mathbf{b}_A+\mathbf{s}+z\mathbf{a}$, where
$\mathbf{s}$ is their transverse separation. Projecting a point on shower
$B$ at $z_j$ onto the layer of a point on shower $A$ at $z_i$ gives distance:
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf{r}_A(z_i)-\frac{z_i}{z_j}\mathbf{r}_B(z_j)
 =\mathbf{b}_A\frac{z_j-z_i}{z_j}-\frac{z_i}{z_j}\mathbf{s}.
 \label{eq:crossshowerprojection}
\end{equation}
The first term is the previously explained projective distance error;
the second represents the true separation of the pair. For suitable shower positions, these terms partly cancel. Points from
different showers can then appear close in the projected plane even while
points along one shower are pushed apart.

\subsection{Which layer gaps produce the merging?}

Because the projection offset grows with $\Delta z$, higher $d$ links bridge
$s$ at smaller $\R$ and cross first. Shorter-gap links $d=1,2$ happen as $\R$ increases.

The two-photon study therefore shows why tuning must consider both shower
completeness and cross-shower links. Recovering energy in larger Tracksters
alone is not sufficient.
